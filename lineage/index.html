Rogue

1980·Michael Toy, Glenn Wichman, Ken Arnold
Rogue

In 1980, Michael Toy and Glenn Wichman, students at UC Santa Cruz, wrote a single Unix program that built a different underground maze every time you played it: a fresh layout of rooms and corridors each run, with no saves once you died. The display used nothing but characters -- @ for you, a letter for each monster. Ken Arnold, author of the curses library, joined later and helped polish it, and it spread across university computers once it was bundled with 4.2BSD. The word "roguelike" comes directly from this game's name.

Highlights

  • Every dungeon is assembled by the computer from scratch, so you never walk the same map twice.
  • Death erases your save. Each run carries real tension because there is no second try.
  • Potions, scrolls, and wands are all unidentified -- you learn what they are only by trying them.
  • The whole game is drawn in plain characters; the @ and each monster's letter slowly become familiar faces.
  • Toy and Wichman, with Jon Lane, founded A.I. Design and turned Rogue into a commercial hit on the IBM PC and Macintosh through Epyx.

Place in the lineage

Rogue is the ancestor of nearly every title on this family tree, from Moria and Hack down to the countless roguelikes that followed. Its descendants branch out both through BSD and through commercial ports, and the entire Japanese-language lineage on this screen also traces back here.

Getting started

  • To actually play, pick Rogue 3.6 or RogueClone2 6.0 from this family tree -- both can be launched directly.
  • Potions, scrolls, and wands stay unknown until you drink, read, or wave them. Don't rush; try one thing at a time.

Rogue → Rogue 3.6

Rogue 3.6 is one of the early BSD-era versions, from the "3.x" line. The map is generated anew every time you play, and each floor you descend reshuffles the rooms, monsters and items completely. Your goal is the Amulet of Yendor on floor 26: grab it and carry it back to the surface. Potions are known only by color, scrolls only by a nonsense title, and wands and rings only by their material -- you find out what they really are by using them. The original ~1981 BSD source was scattered and hard to find for years; several restoration projects have since rebuilt it, and modern ports such as "NewRogue 3.6.4" make it playable today.

Highlights

  • The map is randomly generated every game -- you never see the same dungeon twice.
  • The goal is simple: go down to floor 26 for the Amulet of Yendor and bring it back up.
  • Potions, scrolls, wands, and rings are all unidentified; their colors and materials are randomized every game.
  • Monsters are drawn as single letters, and deeper floors mix in tougher creatures under the same letters.

Place in the lineage

This is Rogue's most direct child on the tree, closest in spirit to the version that spread through universities via BSD Unix, largely unaltered from the original. From here, 5.2 and 5.3 renamed several monsters and cleaned up the save format on the way to 5.4.4.

Getting started

  • Hunger kills regardless of how strong you are. Look for food before you look for more dungeon.
  • Stepping into a dark room, you never know what is waiting -- lean into that tension.
  • Using an unidentified wand or ring is a small gamble; trial is the only way to learn what it does.

Rogue Clone

1986·Tim Stoehr
Rogue ⇢ Rogue Clone

Rogue Clone is a clean-room reimplementation written by Tim Stoehr in 1986, built without touching a line of the original Rogue's code -- Stoehr rebuilt it from watching how the game played. That gives it no license relationship to the original. After being posted to net.sources.games, it was folded into 4.3BSD-Tahoe in 1987, and it became the basis for the "rogue" command bundled with NetBSD, DragonFly BSD, and older FreeBSD/OpenBSD releases.

Highlights

  • A clean-room reimplementation that reuses none of the original Rogue source -- rebuilt purely from observed gameplay.
  • Folded into 4.3BSD-Tahoe in 1987, becoming the ancestor of the "rogue" command on many BSD systems.

Place in the lineage

A separate lineage from the original Rogue, and the direct ancestor of the BSD-bundled versions. Its improved successor, Rogue Clone II, is where Jun Ota's Japanese translation begins -- the starting point for the entire Japanese branch that runs all the way to RogueClone2 6.0 on this screen.

遊びどころ

太田純の日本語版とRogueClone2 6.0の間をつなぐ、保守と移植の版です。ここでの「メッセージをデータとして分離する」という工夫が、後にRogueClone2 6.0が処理全体をUTF-8化する土台になりました。

Data-separated edition / rogueclone2s

1992·Yasushi Ito, FUNABARA Masao, Naohiro Aota
Rogue ⇢ Rogue Clone → Rogue Clone II → Rogue Clone II (Japanese) → Data-separated edition / rogueclone2s

The data-separated edition (rogueclone2s) began in 1992, when Yasushi Ito of the Kyoto University Microcomputer Club took Jun Ota's Japanese Rogue Clone II and split its message and text data out of the source code. FUNABARA Masao later organized a maintenance release called "rogueclone2s," and Naohiro Aota further adapted it into a UTF-8 edition, "rogueclone2s-utf8" -- a relay of maintainers each carrying the project forward.

Highlights

  • Separated message text from source code, making the Japanese edition easier to maintain and modify.
  • Passed through a relay of maintainers -- Yasushi Ito, then FUNABARA Masao, then Naohiro Aota.

Place in the lineage

A maintenance bridge between Jun Ota's Japanese translation and RogueClone2 6.0. The idea of separating messages into data here laid the groundwork for RogueClone2 6.0's later, complete move to UTF-8.

RogueClone2 6.0 is the current Japanese edition, carried forward through Jun Ota's translation, Yasushi Ito's data-separated edition, FUNABARA Masao's rogueclone2s, and Naohiro Aota's UTF-8 build, finished by Iichiro Suzuki. Start it up and it greets you by name -- "Hello, so-and-so. Welcome to the Caverns of Doom..." -- before sending you after the Amulet of Yendor somewhere in the depths. Earlier versions only made terminal output UTF-8; this one converts the message files and source code too, making the whole pipeline UTF-8 throughout.

Highlights

  • Everything from the opening greeting to item names is in Japanese, stored line by line as message text -- "Amulet of Yendor," "food," "scroll," "staff," and more.
  • Potion colors are randomized Japanese words -- blue, red, green -- and you only learn which is which by drinking one.
  • The fruit you find as food carries a Japanese touch: it is called "kokemomo" (lingonberry).
  • The map is randomly generated every game, and your goal is the Amulet of Yendor on floor 26.

Place in the lineage

Counting from Jun Ota's Japanese translation, four maintainers have carried this lineage forward in turn. After a maintenance edition that split out the data and a UTF-8 edition that converted terminal output, this version finally makes the entire pipeline UTF-8. A Japanese localization effort that began in 1987, in the era of Japanese BBS networks, is still being carried on today, on GitHub -- this is where that long lineage currently stands.

Getting started

  • Always keep an eye on your food supply -- starvation can undo even the strongest character.
  • Potion colors and scroll titles are randomized even in Japanese -- drinking, reading, and waving things is still how you learn what they are.

Hack

1982·Jay Fenlason
Rogue ⇢ Hack

Written in 1982 by high-school student Jay Fenlason and three friends, this dungeon-crawler was inspired by Rogue, which they encountered at UC Berkeley. When Rogue's authors declined to share its source code, they rebuilt the game from memory and description alone, so Hack shares no code with Rogue. It was distributed on the 1982 USENIX tapes and became the starting point for the entire Hack lineage, including NetHack. The original source was lost for years before preservationists recovered it.

Highlights

  • Denied the source code, its authors rebuilt the game purely from memory of how it played — an unusual origin story.
  • Distributed on the 1982 USENIX tapes, it spread among Unix users of the era.
  • What was built here became the starting point for the entire Hack lineage, including NetHack.

Place in the lineage

Inspired by Rogue, this is the first work that started the whole Hack lineage (later NetHack). It shares no code with Rogue, and its original source, once lost, has since been recovered by volunteers.

Hack 1.0

1984·Andries Brouwer
Rogue ⇢ Hack → Hack 1.0

Posted by Andries Brouwer to the Usenet newsgroup net.sources in December 1984, this was the first publicly released version of Hack. Brouwer largely rewrote Fenlason's original, adding features that would stay part of the lineage ever since: shops, treasure zoos and vault rooms, a choice of character class, and a starting dog. Further releases (1.0.1 through 1.0.3) followed through July 1985, and this was many Unix users' first encounter with Hack.

Highlights

  • Buying and selling at shops was introduced here for the first time.
  • Special rooms that persist to this day, like treasure zoos and vaults, first appear here.
  • Players could now pick a class at the start, and a starting dog joined as a pet.

Place in the lineage

Brouwer's near-total rewrite of Fenlason's original. Many mechanics that still exist in modern NetHack — shops, pets, choosing a class — were established here.

NetHack 1.3d

1987·NetHack DevTeam
Rogue ⇢ Hack → Hack 1.0 → NetHack 1.3d

Posted by Mike Stephenson to comp.sources.games in July 1987, this was the first release to call itself "NetHack". It merged the two separate Hack lineages descended from Fenlason and Brouwer, and added new character roles such as Samurai, Priest, Valkyrie, and Healer, spellbooks and spells, fountains and throne rooms, and tougher foes like demons and giants. The "Net" in its name reflects that development was now carried out collaboratively over the internet.

Highlights

  • Roles that are still around today — Samurai, Priest, Valkyrie, Healer — were added here.
  • The core of today's spell system — learning spells from spellbooks — has its origin here.
  • A ritual-like mechanic appears: throwing a sword into a fountain can yield Excalibur.
  • New rooms and foes that block your path, like throne rooms, demons, and giants, were added.

Place in the lineage

This release merged the two separate Hack lineages and established both the name "NetHack" and internet-based collaborative development. The roles and spell mechanics introduced here are still part of NetHack today.

NetHack 3.4.3

2003·NetHack DevTeam
Rogue ⇢ Hack → Hack 1.0 → NetHack 1.3d → NetHack 3.4.3

Released on 8 December 2003, this was the DevTeam's 29th public release — mostly an unglamorous bugfix release, closing an exploit involving gold in shops and fixing several crash bugs. Highlights

  • A number of crash bugs were fixed, making it a long-stable release.
  • With no new official release for 12 years, it became the de facto standard version for a long time.
  • SLASH'EM, UnNetHack, dNetHack, NetHack 4, and many other variants are built on this release.

Place in the lineage

The 12-year gap before the next official release made this version the common foundation for a huge number of variants. Most of the NetHack forks shown on this screen trace back to this 3.4.3.

NetHack 3.6.7

2023·NetHack DevTeam
Rogue ⇢ Hack → Hack 1.0 → NetHack 1.3d → NetHack 3.4.3 → NetHack 3.6.7

NetHack's long-serving stable release, the archetypal "start knowing nothing" roguelike. Build a character from 13 roles (Samurai, Priest, Valkyrie, and more), 5 races (human, elf, dwarf, gnome, orc), and 3 alignments (lawful, neutral, chaotic), then descend in search of the Amulet of Yendor. Potions carry no label, scrolls bear gibberish titles, and wands are known only by their material — you quaff, read, and try things to find out what they do. Released 16 February 2023, it carries forward everything the series has built since NetHack's 1987 debut.

Highlights

  • Potions are unidentified, scrolls have made-up titles, and wands are known only by material — you have to use them, or reason from shop prices and weight, to find out what they are.
  • You have unique last resorts for tight spots: praying to your god, or engraving "Elbereth" in the dust with a weapon to make monsters flinch.
  • Multiple side branches lead different places: the Gnomish Mines, Sokoban, your role's own Quest, and Vlad's Tower.
  • You travel with a pet, feeding it and letting it fight alongside you.
  • Death is permanent — there's no reloading a save, so the tension never really lets up.
  • Dropping a ring into a sink usually reveals exactly what it is (though the ring is destroyed in the process).
  • The endgame goal is to complete your role's Quest, cross the Elemental Planes, and face judgment on the Astral Plane.

Place in the lineage

Part of the lineage running back to NetHack 1.3d in 1987, released 16 February 2023. It's a fix-only release closing a memory-corruption issue in 3.6.2–3.6.6 (related to status highlight colors and corpse-name formatting), with no new gameplay added. Long treated as the definitive version, it is the shared ancestor of most of the variants shown on this screen, such as SLASH'EM, UnNetHack, dNetHack, and EvilHack. A successor, NetHack 5.0.0, was released in 2026.

Getting started

  • It's fine to know nothing at first — a shop's asking price can be a clue to what something really is.
  • Eat before you're starving; collapsing from hunger ends the game right there.
  • Praying is a last resort when things go badly wrong, but don't lean on it too often or your god will grow annoyed.

Moria 4.8

1983·Robert Alan Koeneke
Rogue ⇢ Moria 4.8

Robert Alan Koeneke began writing this game around 1981 at the University of Oklahoma, hooked after playing Rogue. Written first in VMS BASIC and then rewritten in VMS Pascal, it was released as Moria 1.0 in 1983. The goal is to descend to the depths and defeat the Balrog. Eight races and six classes are available, and unlike Rogue, the game keeps a permanent shopping town.

Highlights

  • The town never resets. Dungeon levels regenerate every time you use the stairs, but the six shops in town (general store, armoury, weaponsmith, temple, alchemist, and magic shop) stay in the same place for the whole game.
  • Choose from 8 races and 6 classes; Mages, Rangers, and Rogues learn spells, while Priests and Paladins learn prayers.
  • The deeper you go, the tougher the monsters and the better the treasure, so every descent is a judgment call between pushing on and heading back to town.
  • The goal is simple and direct: reach the bottom and defeat the Balrog.
  • Its source code was released early, making it one of the oldest open-source roguelikes.

Place in the lineage

Inspired by Rogue but built independently, it broke new ground with a persistent shopping town. From here the lineage runs through UMoria, then Angband, and eventually Hengband. Its friend-assisted character generator and early open-source release both shaped the roguelikes that followed.

Getting started

  • Gear up in town before diving deep rather than pushing your luck early.
  • Since death is permanent, avoid fights you're not sure you can win.

Hengband is a Japanese variant in the *band roguelike family descended from Moria and Angband, derived directly from the Japanese edition of ZAngband 2.2.8. Development began around 2000. Victory -- "*WINNER*" -- comes only after descending to the bottom of the 100-floor Iron Hell and defeating the final boss, the Serpent of Chaos; getting there takes more than raw level and gear, it takes your own judgment in the field. It has been kept alive for over 20 years, with updates gathered mainly through GitHub and Discord.

Highlights

  • 37 races times 27 classes times 12 personalities means every character feels genuinely different
  • Start at the Yeek Cave right next to town -- safe except for the Yeek King waiting at its lowest level
  • You can travel the wilderness on foot from town to town; roads and plains are relatively safe, but lava fields and open sea hide dangerous monsters
  • Artifacts marked with a star are one-of-a-kind in the world, and *identify* is what finally reveals their full powers
  • Inscribing an item lets you get a confirmation before accidentally using or dropping it, and you can write your own rules for what gets auto-picked-up
  • Clear all 100 floors of the Iron Hell and defeat the Serpent of Chaos to earn *WINNER* status

Place in the lineage

Its roots trace back to Moria/Angband, through Topi Ylinen's Zangband, the 2.2.8 line maintained by Robert Ruehlmann and others, and a Japanese ZAngband 2.2.8 edition by Itakura, before becoming an independent project called Hengband around 2000. From here branch Tangband, the Touhou fan edition (leading to Gensoband), and the Chengband line (leading to PosChengband, Composband, FrogComposband, and FroxComposband).

Getting started

  • Head for the Yeek Cave first -- it lets you build up gear and levels without much risk
  • Traveling the overworld map burns food, so stock up before leaving town
  • Potions, scrolls, and wands start out unidentified -- drinking, reading, and trying them is how you learn what they do

  • Around 253 unique monsters were given guaranteed great-item drops, making rare gear easier to find

  • The rumor service at the frontier's inn is free, making early information easier to gather

Getting started

  • Keep the 14-day clock in mind and push through the Iron Hell without too many detours
  • The recall and light scrolls handed out in early quests are precious -- use them wisely

Larn

Rogue ⇢ Larn

Larn is a dungeon exploration game created by Noah Morgan in 1986, and its defining feature is a hard time limit. Your daughter has fallen ill with a rare disease, and unless you bring back a cure from the depths within a set number of days, the story ends badly. Unlike the more leisurely descents typical of Rogue-family dungeon crawlers, the constant negotiation with the clock is placed at the very center of the gameplay. larn.org hosts a history page, and an actively maintained browser-playable version is still being updated today. A single shopping trip in the early town can end up deciding whether you live or die deep below.

Highlights

  • A clear time-limited mission: bring back a cure for your daughter's illness within a set number of days
  • A constant trade-off between time-consuming actions (resting, shopping, detours) and how fast you can push deeper
  • A separate dangerous area deep in the dungeon (a volcanic cave) gives the exploration real breadth
  • A pioneering work for 1986 in building the entire goal structure around an explicit time limit
  • Even shopping and preparing in town costs time, making the planning before a descent part of the game itself

Place in the lineage

Larn shares no direct code with Rogue but is one of the independent lines it inspired. It sits at the very root of a lineage that branched in 1987 when Phil Cordier added classes and extra dungeon levels to create ULarn, which was later itself forked into ReLarn.

Getting started

  • Keep an eye on the remaining days at all times; if you're behind, cut detours short and prioritize going deeper
  • It's fine to expect an early run to end without delivering the cure -- treat your first playthrough as learning the town and early levels

ULarn

  • Adds extra dungeon levels and new weapons, giving more ground to explore than the original
  • Keeps the original's core goal structure -- racing the clock to find a cure
  • Forks such as the joshbressers version on GitHub are still actively maintained today
  • The larger dungeon gives each class more room to play to its strengths than the original did

Place in the lineage

A direct extension of Larn that keeps its time-limited cure-hunting mission while expanding it with classes and a larger dungeon. A fork of ULarn 1.5 that leaves core gameplay untouched while modernizing code maintainability and the interface. One of the independent lines Rogue inspired, but because its source was never released, no other version has branched from it, leaving it an isolated endpoint on this lineage chart. Its story-first design became a reference point for many later roguelikes that chose to bring in more narrative.

Getting started

  • Check race/class compatibility up front and pick a combination close to how you want to play
  • Pay attention to how quests connect to each other, not just individual runs -- it deepens the sense of the world
  • It's currently sold on Steam, so you can start without worrying about setting anything up yourself
